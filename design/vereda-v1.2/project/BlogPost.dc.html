Power outages in Cuba: what to expect and how we plan around them

Twenty-hour apagones are the baseline this year, not the exception. Here's what a cut looks like from inside a casa, what a generator does and doesn't run, and the four things we do before you arrive.

Why the grid fails

Cuba's power plants are old, fuel is short, and demand peaks in the evening when everyone cooks. The national utility publishes a daily deficit and provinces take turns going dark. In 2025 the island had several total blackouts lasting more than a day; in 2026 planned cuts of 12 to 20 hours outside Havana are normal in the hot months.

None of this is a secret in Cuba. Your hosts plan their day around it: cooking early, charging phones when the light is on, keeping a torch by the bed. You will too, and it is easier than it sounds.

What a cut looks like from inside a casa

Usually the schedule is known a day ahead; Yamilé will tell you at breakfast that the light goes at four. The fan stops, the fridge hums off, and the street gets quieter. If the casa has a generator it starts within a minute and the essentials come back. If it has a battery, the lights and the Wi-Fi router stay on but the air conditioning doesn't.

Evenings are the hardest part: it is hot, and there is less to do. Our travelers' tip, repeated in every review that mentions outages, is to be out. Dinner at a paladar with its own generator, music at the Casa de la Música, a walk along the Malecón. The casa is for sleeping, and a fan on a generator is enough for that.

"Two nights without power, ten hours each. The generator kept the fan on and Ana messaged every morning. Honest, tiring, worth it. Knowing in advance is what made it OK."

Marion T., Lyon · Viñales, February 2026 · verified review

What a generator does and doesn't run

  • Runs: lights, fans, the fridge, phone charging, the router. Usually for 4 to 8 hours a night; fuel for generators is also rationed.
  • Doesn't run: air conditioning in most casas, electric showers, kettles. Premium stays with 24-hour generators are the exception and cost accordingly.
  • Batteries and solar: quieter, no fuel, but smaller. Good for lights, charging and the router for 6 to 8 hours.
  • Hot water: most casas use tanks heated when the grid is up. Shower early.

What we do before you arrive

  1. We only book stays with backup power, verified on a visit: generator with fuel, battery, or solar. It's a filter on the listing and a line on every stay card.
  2. We lend a 20,000 mAh power bank per room and put a headlamp in your welcome kit.
  3. We schedule the important things in the morning, when the grid and the signal are most reliable, and we check in with you by WhatsApp each morning while you're in Cuba.
  4. If a cut runs past 12 hours and the casa can't cope, we move you (Hurricane Melissa in October 2025 was the rehearsal). Mid-trip disruption that costs you a night is refunded pro rata and you get free rebooking within 12 months.

What you can do

  • Bring your own power bank too, and a torch. Charge everything whenever there's light.
  • Download the offline kit and offline maps before you fly; the router won't help when the tower is down.
  • Keep cash in small notes: card terminals don't exist and a shop without light still sells water.
  • Carry a printed itinerary and your emergency card. Paper doesn't need charging.
  • Ask your host about the schedule each morning, and plan the evening out.

Why it's still worth it

Because the outage is also when the street comes alive: neighbours on the porch, dominoes under a single bulb on a battery, someone with a guitar. We won't romanticise it, Cubans don't, and it is a real hardship for the people who live with it every day. But if you understand the reality before you go, you can handle the inconvenience, and what you can't handle is being surprised. That sentence came from a traveler, and it is the whole reason this page exists.
